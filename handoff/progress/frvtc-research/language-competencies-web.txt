L2: # Requests, offers and invitations
L3: 
L4: 【1†Add favourite】
L5: 
L6: Level: beginner
L7: 
L8: ## Requests
L9: 
L10: We use could you … and would you … as polite ways of telling or asking someone to do something:
L11: 
L12: > Could you take a message, please?
L13: > Would you carry this for me, please?
L14: 
L15: can and will are less polite:
L16: 
L17: > Can you take a message, please?
L18: > Will you carry this for me, please?
L19: ## Offers and invitations
L20: 
L21: We use can I … to make offers:
L22: 
L23: > Can I help you?
L24: > Can I do that for you?
L25: 
L26: We can also use shall I …:
L27: 
L28: > Shall I help you with that?
L29: > Shall I call you on your mobile?
L30: 
L31: We sometimes say I can ... or I could ... or I'll (I will) ... to make an offer:
L32: 
L33: > I can do that for you if you like.
L34: > I could give you a lift to the station.
L35: > I'll do that for you if you like.
L36: > I'll give you a lift to the station.
L37: 
L38: We use would you like (to) ... for invitations:
L39: > Would you like to come round tomorrow?
L40: > Would you like another drink?
L41: 
L42: Level: advanced
L43: 
L44: We can use you must … or we must … for a very polite invitation:
L45: 
L46: > You must come round and see us.
L47: > We must meet again soon.

L161: Hi g-ssan,
L162: 
L163: No, we can use can, could or would in that sentence, but not may.
L164: The meaning of 'may' is about permission - giving or receiving permission to do something. Some requests do involve permission, and you can use 'May' if you are requesting permission to do something yourself. For example, you can ask May I borrow your book? That is a request, and it is also asking for permission to do something, so 'May' is fine.
L165: 
L166: I hope that helps to understand it.

L128: # CEFR descriptors search
L129: 
L130: The CEFR provides a common basis for the elaboration of language syllabuses, curriculum guidelines, examinations, textbooks, etc. across Europe.
L131: It describes in a comprehensive way what language learners have to learn to do in order to use a language for communication and what knowledge and skills they have to develop so as to be able to act effectively.  The description also covers the cultural context in which language is set.  The Framework also defines levels of proficiency which allow learners' progress to be measured at each stage of learning and on a life-long basis.
L132: 
L133: Communicative Language Activities
L134: ### Level C2
L135: 
L136: 
L137: 
L138: Scale: Listening as a member of live audience
L139: 
L140: Descriptors:
L141: 
L142:   * Can follow specialised lectures and presentations employing colloquialism, regional usage or unfamiliar terminology
L143:   * Can make appropriate inferences when links or implications are not made explicit
L144:   * Can get the point of jokes or allusions in a presentation
L145: ### Level A1
L146: 
L147: 
L148: 
L149: Scale: Understanding an interlocutor
L150: 
L151: Descriptors:
L152: 
L153:   * Can understand everyday expressions aimed at the satisfaction of simple needs of a concrete type, delivered directly to him/her in clear, slow and repeated speech by a sympathetic speaker
L154:   * Can understand questions and instructions addressed carefully and slowly to him/her and follow short, simple directions
L155: ### Level A1
L156: 
L157: 
L158: 
L159: Scale: Online conversation and discussion
L160: 
L161: Descriptors:
L162:   * Can write very simple messages and personal online postings as a series of very short sentences about hobbies, likes/dislikes, etc., relying on the aid of a translation tool
L163:   * Can use formulaic expressions and combinations of simple words to post short positive and negative reactions to simple online postings and their embedded links and media, and can respond to further comments with standard expressions of thanks and apology.
L164: ### Level A1
L165: 
L166: 
L167: 
L168: Scale: Processing text in writing
L169: 
L170:   * summarising the main points in a source text;
L171:   * collating such information and arguments from different sources;
L172:   * recognising and clarifying to the recipient the intended audience, the purpose and viewpoint of the original.
L173: 
L174: Descriptors:
L175:   * Can, with the help of a dictionary, render (in Language B) simple phrases (written in Language A), but may not always select the appropriate meaning
L176:   * Can copy out single words and short texts presented in standard printed format

L178: Communicative Language Competences
L179: ### Level Pre A1
L180: 
L181: 
L182: 
L183: Scale: General linguistic range
L184: 
L185: Descriptors:
L186: 
L187:   * Can use isolated words and basic expressions in order to give simple information about himself/herself.
L188: 
L189: ### Level A1
L190: 
L191: 
L192: 
L193: Scale: General linguistic range
L194: 
L195: Descriptors:
L196: 
L197:   * Has a very basic range of simple expressions about personal details and needs of a concrete type.
L198:   * Can use some basic structures in one-clause sentences with some omission or reduction of elements
L199: 
L200: Communicative Language Strategies
L201: ### Level A2
L202: 
L203: 
L204: 
L205: Scale: Identifying cues and inferring (spoken&written)
L206: 
L207: Descriptors:
L208: 
L209:   * Can exploit format, appearance and typographic features in order to identify the type of text: news story, promotional text, article, textbook, chat or forum, etc.
L210:   * Can exploit numbers, dates, names, proper nouns, etc. to identify the topic of a text.
L211:   * Can deduce the meaning and function of unknown formulaic expressions from their position in a written text (e.g. at the beginning or end of a letter).
