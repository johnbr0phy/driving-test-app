# AP English Language and Composition (`aplang`): sources

All 202 items and all 22 stimuli (8 nonfiction passages, 14 student drafts) are original. Each passage is a
practice text written for Tigertest; its label ("A speech by a library trustee to a city council, 1876
(practice text)") describes the kind of writer, not a real person, and no real speech, essay or document is
quoted. The drafts are written as student work with numbered sentences, like the CED's writing sets. No College
Board released exam, CED sample question, AP Classroom item or commercial prep item was used or paraphrased.
Facts inside drafts (heat as the deadliest US weather, Repair Cafe's start in the Netherlands in 2009, the sense
of thirst weakening with age, buried creeks "daylighted" by several cities, the winter bird count's start in 1900
as an alternative to a holiday hunt, U.S. railroad standard time from
November 1883, the federal law letting old lighthouses pass to local groups, lawns as the largest irrigated
"crop", bystander CPR doubling or tripling survival) are general knowledge stated loosely, as a student draft
would. The drill passages on standard time and inoculation describe 1883-84 and the 1721 smallpox epidemic in
general terms.

## Format (checked 1 November 2026)
- College Board, AP Central, "AP English Language and Composition Exam":
  https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/courses/ap-english-language-and-composition/exam
  - Fully digital, in the Bluebook app.
  - Section I: 45 multiple-choice questions, 1 hour, 45% of the score: 23 to 25 reading questions on nonfiction
    texts and 20 to 22 writing questions that ask students to "read like writers and consider revisions to
    stimulus texts".
  - Section II: 3 free-response questions (synthesis, rhetorical analysis, argument), 2 hours 15 minutes
    including a 15-minute reading period, 55%.
  - The page announces no format change for 2027 (exam date May 12, 2027, not stated on the landing).
- College Board, "AP English Language and Composition Course and Exam Description", effective fall 2024
  (c) 2024, still the edition linked from AP Central on the date checked:
  https://apcentral.collegeboard.org/media/pdf/ap-english-language-and-composition-course-and-exam-description.pdf
  (downloaded 1 November 2026)
  - Exam overview (p. 107): 3 hours 15 minutes; 45 MCQ (reading 23-25, writing 20-22), 45%, 60 minutes;
    3 FRQ, 55%, 2 hours 15 minutes including a 15-minute reading period, 40 minutes recommended per essay.
  - Skill category weights in Section I (p. 108): 1 Rhetorical Situation, reading 11-14%; 2 Rhetorical
    Situation, writing 11-14%; 3 Claims and Evidence, reading 13-16%; 4 Claims and Evidence, writing 11-14%;
    5 Reasoning and Organization, reading 13-16%; 6 Reasoning and Organization, writing 11-14%; 7 Style,
    reading 11-14%; 8 Style, writing 11-14%.
  - Sets (p. 109): five sets: 1 and 2 reading, 11-14 questions each; 3 and 4 writing, 7-9 each; 5 writing, 4-6.
  - Sample questions (pp. 114-118): four choices (A) to (D); revision items reproduce the sentence with the
    text under revision and give "(as it is now)" as choice (A).
- No penalty for wrong answers: AP Students FAQ, "Will I lose points if I answer a multiple-choice question
  incorrectly?": https://apstudents.collegeboard.org/faqs/will-i-lose-points-if-i-answer-multiple-choice-question-incorrectly
- AP scores are reported 1 to 5; the composite-to-score conversion is set each year and not published as a fixed
  percent. Tigertest's goals are its own targets.

Not confirmed, so not stated: the length of real passages in Section I, how many questions of each skill appear
in a given set, and any score cut.

## Modelling choices
- Section I only, full length and pace: 45 questions in 60 minutes, one module, two practice tests, Test 2 held
  out of the drills. The three essays are not modelled.
- Each test is the CED's five sets in order: reading 12 and 12 (inside 11-14), writing 8, 8 and 5 (inside 7-9,
  7-9, 4-6), so 24 reading and 21 writing questions (inside 23-25 and 20-22).
- Per test by skill category (domain): reading 5/7/6/6 (11.1%, 15.6%, 13.3%, 13.3%), writing 5/6/5/5 (11.1%,
  13.3%, 11.1%, 11.1%), each inside its CED range.
- Passages are about 400 words (shorter than many real passages, for phone reading); questions cite
  paragraphs and quote phrases instead of line numbers.
- Revision items keep "(as it is now)" first and locked, Keep/Yes options before Delete/No options, and
  placement options in sentence order, as on the real exam; all other options are shuffled.
- Drill-only items: four reading passages of 12 (3 per reading category) and eight drafts of 8 (2 per writing
  category), so each drill has 17 to 22 items.
- Score is percent correct.

## Overlap check (1 November 2026)
Grepped the SAT, PSAT, ACT, GED, Praxis, AP Psychology, AP U.S. History and AP Biology banks for the same topics.
Three drafts were replaced because a bank already had a close twin: a light-pollution draft (Praxis has one with
the same night-migrating-birds point), a bird-safe glass draft (SAT reading on patterned glass) and a school
composting draft (SAT data item on a cafeteria composting program). Remaining shared topics (protected bike lanes
on the ACT, street trees on the SAT) use different facts and angles.

## Review pass (1 November 2026)
- AP Central exam page re-read on 1 November 2026: fully digital in Bluebook; Section I 45 questions, 1 hour, 45%
  (23-25 reading, 20-22 writing); Section II 3 free-response questions, 2 hours 15 minutes including a 15-minute
  reading period, 55%. Confirmed as stated above.
- Overlap fixes: the lighthouse draft's list of keepers' duties (trimmed wicks, wound the clockwork) matched the ACT
  English Test 2 lighthouse passage almost word for word, so sentence 2 now lists different duties (oil, the lamp from
  dusk to dawn, the fog bell). The bike-lane draft's objection (store owners fear losing parking; customers on foot or
  bike spend as much) matched a Praxis item and a GED letter pair, so it now answers drivers who fear slower traffic.
- Remaining shared topic: Praxis has a short passage on the November 1883 railroad time zones; the drill essay on
  standard time asks about a different argument (who should hold the power to set time), with no item keyed on the
  date or the number of zones.
